# Verifying novelty

"It sounds different" is not evidence of novelty. Neither is "I haven't seen it."

## Search dimensions

- exact terminology, and **synonyms used by other communities** for the same object;
- mathematically **equivalent formulations** (the same operation under a different name);
- the mechanism, not the framing — many methods implement one idea in different language;
- adjacent fields (statistics, information theory, econometrics, ecology) — deformed
  entropies, diversity indices, and concentration measures are re-derived constantly;
- recent preprints, not only published venues;
- classical literature, where the idea may be decades old under other terminology.

## The question to answer

> Has essentially **this exact scientific idea** already been proposed, derived, or
> experimentally demonstrated?

If the answer is uncertain, **label the claim uncertain**. Do not resolve uncertainty in
your own favour.

## Tier your claims

Separating what is genuinely new from what is standard is a strength, not a concession:

| Tier | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Genuinely new** | a result we could not find anywhere, proved and verified |
| **New but elementary** | new as far as we can tell, but follows quickly from standard tools |
| **Standard tool, new application** | the technique is classical; using it *here* is the contribution |
| **Explicit non-claims** | what we are *not* claiming, stated so reviewers need not infer it |

An explicit non-claims list ("we do not claim X is achievable; we do not claim no method
can do Y — we claim methods of class Z cannot, and we measured that") pre-empts the most
damaging referee objections and costs nothing but honesty.

## Awkward-fit honesty

If your best mathematics characterises something you later show does not matter, say so and
reposition it as the *explanation* rather than the headline. Hiding the tension is worse: a
sharp reviewer will find it, and then everything else looks suspect too.
